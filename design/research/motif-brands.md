# Motif brands: recurring characters without clutter

References for Verity's recurring yellow smiley: sites and brand systems where one character or shape keeps coming back and stays charming, with notes on placement, density and restraint. All URLs were checked on 2026-10-26. Observations come from page structure (image placement, alt text, filenames) plus the published guidelines.

1. **Headspace, meditation library**: https://www.headspace.com/meditation
   Simple smiling faces and suns ("Smiling sun facing flower", "Green peaceful smiling face", "Smiling blue face") show up only as course thumbnails. Each one gets its own color and pose, and none appear in the running text. This is the closest match to a smiley in a garden: the face marks an entry point and never decorates the copy.

2. **The Go website and gopher**: https://go.dev/ (backstory: https://go.dev/blog/gopher)
   On the homepage the gopher appears about four times: a banner, two section illustrations (climbing a ladder, flying a biplane) and a bust in the footer. Each pose acts out the section it sits in, and the docs pages carry no gopher at all. Few appearances, every one doing a job.

3. **Rust's Ferris in The Rust Book**: https://doc.rust-lang.org/book/ch00-00-introduction.html (art: https://rustacean.net/)
   Ferris shows up next to code blocks only when it means something: confused for "does not compile", hands up for "panics", shrugging for "wrong behavior". The official site, https://www.rust-lang.org/, leaves Ferris off the homepage entirely. So the mascot works as a signal, which makes each appearance worth noticing.

4. **Mailchimp brand assets (Freddie)**: https://mailchimp.com/about/brand-assets/
   The guidelines call for Freddie to always sit next to the wordmark, always winking, with "plenty of space" around him. The current homepage, https://mailchimp.com/, has no Freddie illustrations at all. This shows that one fixed expression plus generous clear space can carry a whole brand. It also backs our "steady smile, no wink or blink" rule.

5. **Duolingo design hub (Duo)**: https://blog.duolingo.com/hub/design/
   Duo is everywhere, but his emotional range (hopeful, proud, even melting in the widget work) is what does the work. The art changes the expression and keeps the shape. Treat this as the upper limit on density: Duo works because he behaves like a product character, and our homepage mascot shouldn't try to.

6. **GitHub Octodex**: https://octodex.github.com/ (and https://github.com/404)
   The Octocat's many variants (#1 to #162) are kept in a separate gallery, and the main product pages (for example /features/copilot) carry none of them. The character turns up in "off-duty" places like the gallery, the 404 page and swag. Putting variety in the margins keeps the main path clean.

7. **Sentry homepage**: https://sentry.io/welcome/
   The main sections show product UI. The cartoon of the logo as a distressed character only appears near the newsletter / "Fix It" area at the bottom. Humor goes below the fold, the product stays above it.

8. **Oatly**: https://www.oatly.com/
   The site and packaging lean on hand-drawn type and doodles, more a tone of voice than a character. This is a counterexample: it's dense and noisy on purpose. Useful for knowing how far is too far. Note: the page structure didn't confirm any visual specifics, so check by eye.

**Takeaways for Verity**
- One hero appearance, then 2–4 small, purposeful repeats (section markers, status/empty states, footer). Don't scatter them as decoration.
- Change the pose, color or context, never the face. A fixed smile is a strength (Mailchimp, Headspace).
- Keep the smiley out of body copy and code samples, unless it carries meaning the way Ferris does.
- Put playful variants in the margins: the 404 page, footer, below the fold.
